## Output

### 1. Mass affluent vs. Premium vs. Private banking

**1. General definition.** *Mass affluent* describes a type of customer: people with more income or savings than the average retail customer, but less than very wealthy clients. *Premium* is a service package a bank sells or gives to selected customers. *Private banking* is the most intensive service for wealthy or complex clients.

**2. In banking.** "Mass affluent" is a commercial label, not a legal category. Each bank and survey uses its own thresholds. For example, EY's 2025 survey uses US$250,000–US$1m in assets, while Tatra banka (Slovakia) advertises Premium mainly from €2,500 monthly income or €40,000 managed assets. In Serbia, our expert mentioned Premium from a salary of about 150,000 RSD per month (approximate, from the transcript). Raiffeisen Bank Hungary's Premium and private-banking entry conditions were not checked. **This requires verification.**

**3. Key distinctions.** Mass affluent is *who the customer is*. Premium is *what the bank offers*. Some mass-affluent people are Premium customers, some are not (they use a standard account, or bank mainly elsewhere). Being Premium does not make someone a "professional client" under MiFID II; that classification has its own rules. Private banking is our upper boundary.

**4. Practical example.** In a Budapest street interview, someone with a good salary and savings says "I'm not Premium". They can still be mass affluent. If we only interview Premium customers, we miss the people the bank has not yet recognised, who may be the ones moving money elsewhere.

**5. How to measure.** Segment size = number of customers meeting our chosen definition (income, savings or both) in one market. Track it from the bank's segmentation data (CRM / BI). For interviews, use a simple screening question instead of amounts.

### 2. Financial education vs. Guidance vs. Investment advice

**1. General definition.** *Education* explains how money things work for everyone. *Guidance* helps a person understand their options and the next step for their situation, without telling them which product to buy. *Advice* is a personal recommendation: "for you, this investment".

**2. In banking.** Under MiFID II, investment advice means a personal recommendation about specific financial instruments. It needs a suitability assessment and a suitability report explaining why the recommendation fits the client. Education and general guidance do not have these requirements, but they must be fair, clear and not misleading. The exact line between guidance and advice depends on how something is worded and presented. **Where our idea sits on this line requires checking with compliance.**

**3. Key distinctions.** Education: "What is a fund?" Guidance: "People with your goal and a one-year horizon usually look at savings rather than shares; here are your options." Advice: "Put 60% in fund X and 40% in fund Y." Product information or sales talk is not the same as advice, even if it feels personal to the customer.

**4. Practical example.** Our working goal says "guidance". A short in-app module explaining risk and fees, followed by a guided check of the customer's goals, could help many people at low cost. If the customer then wants a concrete portfolio, they move into the regulated advice process (robo or banker). Knowing this line lets us design something affordable without breaking rules.

**5. How to measure.** Reach (how many customers used education / guidance / advice), conversion from guidance to advice, and customer understanding (a short comprehension question after the content). Sources: app analytics, CRM records of advice sessions.

### 3. Suitability assessment (MiFID II) vs. Appropriateness test

**1. General definition.** Both are checks a bank does before an investment. One asks "is this right for *you*?", the other asks "do you *understand* this product?".

**2. In banking.** *Suitability* (MiFID II Article 25(2)) applies to investment advice and portfolio management. The bank must collect information on the client's knowledge and experience, financial situation (including ability to bear losses) and investment objectives (including risk tolerance). It also covers sustainability preferences (ESMA guidelines). *Appropriateness* (Article 25(3)) applies when the customer chooses a product themselves without advice. The bank checks only knowledge and experience, and warns if the product seems inappropriate. For some simple products bought on the customer's own initiative, "execution-only" can apply with no test. Hungary, Czech Republic, Slovakia and Romania apply MiFID II as EU members. Serbia is outside the EU and has its own capital-market law. **The Serbian rules require verification.**

**3. Key distinctions.** Suitability is wider (goals, money situation, loss capacity) and leads to a recommendation. Appropriateness is narrower (knowledge only) and leads to a warning, not a recommendation. Customers often see both as "the questionnaire" or "paperwork".

**4. Practical example.** Our expert said mass customers mainly get non-advisory service ("here are 15 funds, you choose"). In the EU, that means an appropriateness test, not suitability. The customer may *think* they got advice. This matters for H1: "not personalised" may partly be because no advice was given at all.

**5. How to measure.** Completion rate of the questionnaire, time to complete, drop-off per question, and the share of customers whose profile is outdated (for example, older than one year). Source: digital journey analytics, advisory system logs.

### 4. Robo-advice vs. Hybrid advice vs. Self-directed investing

**1. General definition.** *Robo-advice* is advice or portfolio management produced by an algorithm through a digital channel. *Hybrid advice* combines digital steps with access to a human adviser. *Self-directed investing* means the customer chooses and buys investments on their own.

**2. In banking.** Robo-advice is still regulated advice. The algorithm must meet suitability rules, and ESMA expects firms to test and supervise it. Raiffeisenbank Czech Republic publicly documents automated advice in Raiffeisen investice, with employee involvement on request. It is non-independent, limited to selected instruments, and a one-off recommendation (disclosure dated 03.11.2025). Robo-advice does not have to use generative AI; it usually uses fixed rules.

**3. Key distinctions.** Robo = algorithm recommends. Hybrid = algorithm or app does simple steps, a human helps at key moments. Self-directed = no recommendation, customer decides (an appropriateness test may apply). A chatbot that answers questions is not robo-advice unless it gives personal recommendations.

**4. Practical example.** H3 says customers want digital for simple steps and a human for important decisions. That is hybrid. If the Czech robo already exists, our idea could be the missing "human at the right moment" step, rather than building a new robo.

**5. How to measure.** Share of investment customers per channel (robo / hybrid / banker / self-directed), how often robo users request a human, and completion from start to funded investment per channel. Source: digital analytics and CRM.

### 5. Risk tolerance vs. Capacity for loss

**1. General definition.** *Risk tolerance* is how much up-and-down in value a person is *willing* to accept emotionally. *Capacity for loss* is how much loss a person can *financially afford* without hurting their life plans.

**2. In banking.** Both are part of the MiFID II suitability assessment: risk tolerance under "investment objectives", ability to bear losses under "financial situation". Banks usually ask scenario questions, for example "What would you do if your investment fell 20%?" Our expert suggested exactly this question (sell all / do nothing / buy more).

**3. Key distinctions.** A confident customer may *want* risk but cannot afford a loss (for example, the money is needed for a flat deposit next year). A careful customer may be able to afford losses but feels uncomfortable. Advice must respect the lower of the two. Risk tolerance is a feeling and can change after a market drop. Capacity is about numbers.

**4. Practical example.** A young Budapest professional says "I'm fine with risk". But all their savings are their emergency reserve. Capacity for loss is low, so a high-risk fund would be wrong. If our guidance only asks about feelings, it could push people into unsuitable products.

**5. How to measure.** Risk-profile score from the questionnaire; capacity estimated from income stability, emergency reserve and upcoming spending. Over time: how many customers sell in panic after a market drop (behaviour data).

### 6. Investment horizon vs. Liquidity

**1. General definition.** *Investment horizon* is how long until the customer needs the money. *Liquidity* is how quickly money can be turned into cash without a big loss or penalty.

**2. In banking.** Horizon drives the choice of product: short horizons suit deposits or money-market funds, long horizons can carry more market risk. Our expert called horizon "the number one input". Liquidity applies both to the customer (do they have an emergency reserve?) and to the product (can it be sold quickly? are there exit fees?).

**3. Key distinctions.** A customer can have a long horizon but still need liquidity for emergencies. A product can be liquid (sell any day) but still risky in the short term, because its value may be down on the day you need the money. "I can withdraw it any time" is not the same as "I will get back what I put in".

**4. Practical example.** The expert's two common mistakes: a risky fund for money needed in one year (for an apartment), and a cash fund for a five-year goal (missing returns). In Hungary, retail government securities and the long-term investment account (TBSZ) have their own holding periods and rules. **These local products and their terms require verification.** Any guidance we design should ask "When do you need this money?" first.

**5. How to measure.** Share of customers with a stated horizon in their profile; mismatch rate (short horizon but high-risk product); emergency reserve as months of spending. Sources: advisory records, account data (with permitted use).

### 7. Share of wallet vs. Churn vs. Hidden defection

**1. General definition.** *Share of wallet* is the part of a customer's total financial business that sits with our bank. *Churn* is when a customer leaves completely (closes the account). *Hidden defection* is when a customer stays, but moves new or extra business (for example, investments) to another provider.

**2. In banking.** Banks usually track churn well, because an account closes. Hidden defection is harder to see: the salary account remains, so the customer looks loyal. Bain describes this mechanism, with investments as one exposed area (2019 article; its numbers are not a current benchmark). EY's 2025 survey found 29% of wealth clients *planned* to change their main provider within three years. That is an intention in a global mixed sample, not an RBI churn rate.

**3. Key distinctions.** Churn = customer lost. Hidden defection = business lost, customer kept. Share of wallet is the measure that shows hidden defection. Money leaving is not always defection: it may be spending, a house purchase or a valid life change.

**4. Practical example.** A Premium customer in Budapest gets their salary at Raiffeisen, but buys government securities through the state channel and ETFs through a fintech app. The bank sees an active, "happy" customer with falling balances. Asking in interviews "Where else do you save or invest?" shows this.

**5. How to measure.** Share of wallet = assets with our bank ÷ customer's total assets (total usually from surveys or customer self-report, since the bank cannot see other banks). Churn rate = customers who closed ÷ customers at start of period. Hidden defection signals: regular outflows to brokers or other banks, falling deposit balances while salary continues. Sources: transaction data (permitted use only), surveys, interviews.

### 8. AUM vs. Deposits

**1. General definition.** *AUM (assets under management)* is the value of investments a provider manages or holds for clients. *Deposits* are money in current and savings accounts.

**2. In banking.** Deposits are on the bank's balance sheet and the bank earns mainly from the interest margin. AUM (funds, portfolios, securities) usually earns fees. Banks often want to move "excess" deposits into investments, but that is only right when it fits the customer's goals and horizon.

**3. Key distinctions.** AUM is not all the customer's money at the bank. Deposits are not "wasted" money: a cash reserve can be the right advice. Growing AUM at the cost of suitability is a conflict of interest our guardrails warn about.

**4. Practical example.** Our expert said many customers keep savings in deposits and the bank used signals like "more than 200 (unit unclear) left in the account for three months" to find fund prospects. Our idea must help customers choose well, not just raise AUM.

**5. How to measure.** AUM in the segment, deposits in the segment, and the share of segment customers holding any investment. Net flows into and out of investments per month. The revenue effect needs bank-specific margin data; any number we use before that is an assumption.

### 9. Personalisation vs. Segmentation

**1. General definition.** *Segmentation* means grouping customers by shared features (income, age, assets) and treating each group the same way. *Personalisation* means adjusting the service to one person's goals, situation and preferences.

**2. In banking.** Segmentation decides who gets which service tier (standard, Premium, private). Personalisation in wealth advice means the recommendation reflects *this* customer's goals, horizon, loss capacity and life situation. Data-based personalisation must respect GDPR and the bank's permitted use of data.

**3. Key distinctions.** A Premium card is segmentation, not personalisation. A campaign email with your name in it is not personalised advice. Real personalisation starts from the customer's goal, not from the product the bank wants to sell.

**4. Practical example.** This is our hypothesis H1: at least 7 of 10 mass-affluent customers will say their advice is not sufficiently personalised. To test it, we must ask about *their* last advice experience. Then we separate "I was put in a group" from "they understood my situation". H4 (advisers lack data and tools) may be one reason personalisation is weak.

**5. How to measure.** Customer-reported fit ("How well did the advice fit your goals?", scale 1–5), share of advice sessions with documented personal goals, and time an adviser needs to prepare. Sources: interviews, surveys, CRM.

---

### 10. Goal-based planning

**1. General definition.** Planning that starts from what a person wants to achieve (a flat in five years, retirement, children's education) and works backwards to how much to save and how to invest.

**2. In banking.** Goal-based planning links goal, amount, date and priority to a savings plan and investments. It also checks cash reserves, debt and protection. The strategy paper (generic, not RBI-verified) suggests reporting goal progress, risk, contribution adequacy and next action together. Serbia's public Premium pages advertise planning across saving, investing, financing, protection and retirement. How often customers actually receive it is unknown.

**3. Key distinctions.** Goal-based planning is not the same as a risk questionnaire followed by a product. It is also not the same as showing portfolio performance. "+5% this year" does not tell you if you are on track for your goal. It can be education, guidance or advice, depending on whether it ends in a personal product recommendation.

**4. Practical example.** A customer saving for a flat in Budapest in three years gets a simple goal check: "You need X per month; keep this part in savings because of the short horizon." This is useful even if no investment is sold, and it may build trust, which is our working goal.

**5. How to measure.** Share of customers with at least one documented goal, share with a goal review in the last 12 months, and "on track" rate. Customer confidence before and after ("How sure are you that you will reach your goal?"). Sources: app, CRM, short surveys.

## What this means for our challenge

- **H1 personalisation:** before asking "was the advice personal?", check *whether advice was given at all*. Many mass customers may have had non-advisory service with an appropriateness test only. Our interview notes should record which one they had.
- **H2 knowledge and confidence:** the gap between risk tolerance and capacity for loss, and the gap between horizon and liquidity, are exactly what customers find hard. Education and guidance can cover them without a regulated recommendation.
- **H3 hybrid channels:** use the words carefully. Robo, hybrid and self-directed are different services. A "call a human" step inside an existing robo (Czech example) is hybrid, and may be cheaper than a new tool.
- **H4 advisor data and tools:** personalisation needs goal, horizon and loss-capacity data that advisers may not have to hand. Ask advisers which of these fields they lack.
- **Team decision needed:** agree one definition of "mass affluent" for the chosen market, and use a simple screening question in Budapest. Premium status alone is not the definition.
