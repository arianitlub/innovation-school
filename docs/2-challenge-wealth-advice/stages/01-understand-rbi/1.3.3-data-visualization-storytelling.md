# 1.3.3 Data Visualization & Storytelling — mass-affluent wealth advice

## Filled prompt

```text
I have completed data analysis on our mass-affluent wealth advice journey (service quality, customer retention / share of wallet, advisor efficiency). Now I need to visualize findings and tell a compelling story to stakeholders.

Challenge: How are mass affluent clients served with wealth management advice and where are the gaps? How can we find more ways to offer more value to customers during the process? Working goal: How can we give mass-affluent customers useful, affordable financial guidance that helps them achieve their goals and strengthens their relationship with the bank? Company: Raiffeisen Bank International (RBI), network banks in CEE.

My key findings include:
1. Mass affluent and Premium overlap; advice offers already exist in some RBI markets (public pages, e.g. Serbia Premium planning, Czech automated advice). Evidence: confirmed from public sources.
2. An internal expert reports limited branch time, paperwork, confusing tools, and a need for education and assisted digital journeys. Evidence: reported, single interview (Serbian context, many transcript gaps).
3. EY 2025: 29% of wealth clients plan to change their primary provider within 3 years. Evidence: external survey; intention, not churn.
4. FCA 2024 (UK): 8.6% of adults received investment/pension advice in the last 12 months. Evidence: external, UK only.
5. Hidden defection: customers keep their salary account but invest elsewhere. Evidence: established mechanism (Bain), not measured at RBI.
We have no internal RBI data yet; customer and expert interviews are planned in Budapest 13.–16.10.2026.

Help me create a data storytelling framework:

1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY DASHBOARD
   - What are the 4-6 most important metrics to show at a glance?
   - What visualization types work best for executives? (KPI cards, trend lines, heat maps, comparison charts)
   - How to show "so what?" (impact on business outcomes)
   - Design principles for clarity and impact

2. PROBLEM VISUALIZATION
   - How to visually demonstrate the scale of the problem?
   - Chart types to show:
     * Declining performance trends (line charts)
     * Problem frequency or distribution (bar charts, pareto charts)
     * Process bottlenecks or drop-offs (funnel charts, Sankey diagrams)
     * Segmentation patterns (pie charts, treemaps, heatmaps)

3. ROOT CAUSE VISUALIZATION
   - How to show relationships between variables?
   - How to demonstrate cause-and-effect patterns?
   - Use scatter plots, correlation matrices, fishbone diagrams, waterfall charts?
   - How to make causal relationships clear vs. just correlations?

4. IMPACT QUANTIFICATION VISUALIZATION
   - How to show business impact?
   - How to visualize costs, lost revenue, or inefficiencies?
   - How to show value at risk or opportunity size?
   - Make financial impact crystal clear to leadership

5. COMPARATIVE BENCHMARKING VISUALIZATION
   - How to show our performance vs. competitors or industry standards?
   - How to display internal comparisons (regions, teams, time periods)?
   - Use comparison bars, gap analysis charts, quadrant plots?

6. STORYTELLING STRUCTURE
   Recommend a narrative flow:
   - Opening: What's the business problem or opportunity?
   - Data: What does the data tell us? (key findings)
   - Analysis: Why is this happening? (root causes)
   - Impact: What does this cost us or what are the consequences?
   - Opportunity: What could we achieve if we address this?
   - Call to Action: What do we need to do?

7. POWERPOINT/PRESENTATION STRUCTURE
   Suggest slide sequence with:
   - Recommended chart type for each slide
   - Key message headline for each slide
   - Supporting data points
   - What to emphasize (visual hierarchy)

8. INTERACTIVE DASHBOARD (if applicable)
   - If creating a BI dashboard (Power BI, Tableau, Excel), what layout?
   - What filters or drill-down capabilities to include?
   - How to enable stakeholders to explore data themselves?

9. DATA CREDIBILITY & CONFIDENCE
   - How to show data quality and confidence levels?
   - When to include sample sizes, margins of error, or data limitations?
   - How to address data limitations transparently?

10. COMMON VISUALIZATION MISTAKES TO AVOID
    - What chart types to avoid and why?
    - How to prevent misleading visualizations?
    - Accessibility considerations (color blindness, screen readers)

For each visualization recommendation, provide:
- When to use it (what data/story it tells best)
- How to create it (Excel, PowerPoint, BI tool)
- Example sketch or description
- What makes it effective

OUTPUT FORMAT:
Visualization guide with chart type recommendations, storytelling structure, and slide-by-slide presentation outline. Include design principles and examples.
```

## Output

**Read this first.** The prompt says "I have completed data analysis". We have not. We have five findings of mixed strength and no internal RBI data. So the honest story this week is: **"Here is what we know, how sure we are, and what we will find out in Budapest."** Every chart below must show its evidence label. Any number not in the five findings is **illustrative, not real data**.

**Evidence labels used throughout**

| Label | Meaning | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| 🟢 Confirmed (public) | Checked on an official public page | F1 |
| 🟡 Reported | One person said it; not measured | F2 |
| 🔵 External | Outside survey, other population or country | F3, F4 |
| ⚪ Mechanism | Known pattern, not measured at RBI | F5 |
| ⬜ Unknown | Needs internal data or interviews | Segment size, drop-off, advisor time |

Use shape or text as well as colour (e.g. the word "Reported" on the card), so the labels also work in black and white and for colour-blind readers.

### 1. Executive summary dashboard

**4–6 metrics at a glance (Understand stage version)**

| Card | Value now | Label | Later replaced by |

**Best types for executives:** KPI cards with a one-line "so what" under each; one small comparison bar; text cards where we have words, not numbers. Avoid fake gauges for numbers we do not have.

**"So what?"** Under each card write the consequence for the bank, e.g. under 29%: "If even part of this is real for our segment, investment relationships are at risk before accounts close (not measured at RBI)."

**Design principles:** one message per card; large number, small source line; evidence label always visible; grey for unknowns instead of empty space, so gaps are visible and honest.

*How to create:* PowerPoint shapes (rounded rectangles) or Excel cells with large font. *Why effective:* leadership sees in 10 seconds what is known and what is not.

### 2. Problem visualisation

We cannot yet show the scale of the problem with RBI numbers. Show **where** the problem might be, and leave clearly marked placeholders.

- **Journey map with pain points (main visual now).** Horizontal line of the 8 steps (Trigger → Information → App/branch → Questionnaire → Proposal → Signing → Funding → Review). Under each step, sticky-note style pains from the expert and desk research, each with its label. *When:* we know stages and reported pains but not volumes. *How:* PowerPoint SmartArt or Miro. *Why:* shows the whole experience in one picture.
- **Funnel chart (placeholder).** Same steps as bars getting narrower. This week: grey bars with "data requested". Later: real step counts. *How:* Excel funnel chart (Insert → Charts → Funnel). Sketch: the illustrative funnel in [1.3.2](1.3.2-data-analysis-methods.md) (10,000 → 630, illustrative, not real data).
- **Pareto chart of interview themes (after Budapest).** Bars of how many interviewees named each theme (e.g. "don't understand risk", "no time for branch"), sorted, with a cumulative line. *How:* Excel histogram → Pareto. *Why:* shows the few themes that matter most.
- **Heatmap: segment × journey step (later).** Rows = segments (mass, mass affluent, Premium), columns = steps, colour = drop-off. Use single-hue shading with values printed in cells.
- **Trend lines:** only once we have monthly internal data. Do not draw a "declining" line we have not measured.
- **Pie charts / treemaps:** skip for now. Use a simple stacked bar if we need segment shares later.

### 3. Root cause visualisation

- **Fishbone diagram (main now).** Head: "Customers with money do not get from interest to a suitable action with support". Bones: Customer (knowledge, trust; H2), Process (paperwork, return visits; F2), Channel (app and branch not connected; H3), People (advisor time; H4), Tools/data (complex tools; F2, H4), Offer (one-off advice, e.g. Czech scope; F1). Mark each cause with its evidence label. *How:* PowerPoint lines and text boxes, or Miro template. *Why:* shows many possible causes without pretending we know which is main.
- **Hypothesis evidence chart (after interviews).** Stacked bars per hypothesis H1–H4: Y / N / ? counts, with a vertical pass-mark line at 7/10, 6/10, 7/10. *Why:* makes "validated or not" visible.
- **Scatter plot (later).** E.g. advisor time per customer vs investment uptake per branch. Label it "relationship, not proof of cause".
- **Causal chain diagram** for hidden defection (F5): "Interest in investing → no timely help → invests with another provider → salary stays, investments leave → bank loses relevance". Mark it ⚪ Mechanism, not measured.

**Making correlation vs cause clear:** use the words "linked with" for correlations and "causes" only after an experiment. Put "Why we think so" and "How we will test it" next to each cause.

Waterfall charts and correlation matrices: keep for the business-case stage, when we have numbers.

### 4. Impact quantification visualisation

We have **no measured impact**. Show impact as a clearly labelled scenario.

- **Value-at-risk scenario (illustrative).** From [our research](../../research/02-mass-affluent-customers-bank-services-and-retention.md): 1,000 customers × €50,000 investments; if 10% move all balances, €5m leaves; at an assumed 0.5% net revenue rate, €25,000 a year. **Illustrative, not real data. Neither 10% nor 0.5% is an RBI value.** Visual: a simple bar for "€5m assets moved (scenario)" and a small bar for "€25k annual revenue (scenario)", with a sensitivity table beside it (5%, 10%, 20% moving).
- **Sensitivity table** is better than one big number: it shows the result depends on assumptions.
- **Time-cost visual (illustrative).** The expert's rough example (about 1.5 hours for a small investment) as a stacked bar: "explaining / advising" vs "paperwork / filing". Split unknown; label 🟡 Reported, estimate. Later replace with measured minutes.
- **Opportunity framing:** "If advisors spent less time on paperwork, more time could go to explaining why a proposal fits" (expert's point, reported). Do not convert this into money yet.

*How:* Excel bar chart + table; scenario name in the title, e.g. "Scenario, not forecast". *Why effective:* leadership sees the size of what could be at stake without being misled.

### 5. Comparative benchmarking visualisation

- **Benchmark context cards, not gap bars.** Show 8.6% (UK, FCA 2024) and 29% (global, EY 2025) as separate cards with country, year and definition. Do not draw them next to an RBI bar we do not have.
- **Feature comparison table (public offers).** Rows: RBI Serbia Premium, Raiffeisenbank Czech Republic automated advice, 2–3 competitors in the chosen market (needs verification). Columns: digital advice, human access, regular review, whole-portfolio view, fees explained. Ticks / crosses / "not stated". Only fill from public pages and cite them.
- **Quadrant plot.** X = digital depth, Y = human support. Place public offers. Label "based on public pages, not usage". *Why:* shows where a hybrid model could sit.
- **Internal comparisons (later):** market vs market, Premium vs non-Premium mass affluent, before vs after robo pilot — grouped bars with the same scale.

### 8. Interactive dashboard (later, if internal data arrives)

Not needed this week. If we later get internal data, a simple Excel or Power BI page:
- **Top row:** KPI cards (segment size, share investing, advice reach, review rate, complaints per 1,000 investors).
- **Middle:** journey funnel (left), segment × step heatmap (right).
- **Bottom:** monthly trend with event markers; interview theme Pareto.

### 9. Data credibility and confidence

- **Label every number** with source, year, country and evidence type (table at the top).
- **Always show sample size** for interviews: "7 of 10 in-segment customers", never "70%" alone.
- **10 interviews are a signal, not proof.** Say so on the slide. Margins of error are not meaningful at this size; do not compute them.
- **Uncertain transcript content:** mark the expert's figures as "estimate, reported", and remember the transcript has many `[unclear]` gaps and unreliable speaker labels. Do not attribute quotes to named people.
- **Illustrative numbers:** put "Illustrative, not real data" in the chart title, not only in a footnote.
- **Limitations slide or box:** no internal data; strategy paper generic and original absent; public pages show offers, not usage; external surveys from other populations.
- **Show unknowns** in grey, so missing data is visible rather than hidden.

### 10. Common visualisation mistakes to avoid

- **Placing external benchmarks next to RBI as if comparable** (e.g. UK 8.6% vs a CEE figure). Different definitions and countries.
- **Presenting 29% as churn.** It is stated intention.
- **Percentages without n** for small interview samples.
- **Invented trend lines** or "declining" charts without data.
- **3D, pie charts with many slices, dual axes** — hard to read and easy to mislead.
- **Truncated y-axis** on bar charts making small gaps look big; bars should start at zero.
- **Red/green only** for good/bad. Use a colour-blind-safe palette (e.g. blue/orange), plus labels or icons.
- **Accessibility:** large fonts (min 18 pt on slides), high contrast, alt text on charts in PowerPoint, data labels printed on bars, headline that states the message in words so screen-reader users get the point.
- **Too much on one slide:** one message, one chart.

## What this means for our challenge

- **H1 personalisation:** we have no evidence yet either way. The readout should show H1 as an open question and the Czech "one-off, selected instruments" scope as a reason to ask whether advice covers the whole customer situation.
- **H2 knowledge / confidence:** the expert's call for education is reported only (🟡). After interviews, a Pareto of "what is difficult" themes is the clearest way to show it.
- **H3 hybrid channels:** public offers (🟢) and the reported robo pilot (🟡) suggest digital and human pieces exist. The story is about whether they connect; the journey map and quadrant plot carry that message.
- **H4 advisor data / tools / time:** the strongest current signal (time, paperwork, complex tools), but from a single interview. Present it as a quote card with "single interview", not as a measured fact.
- **Overall:** our Understand readout is a story of "what we know, how sure we are, and what we test next". The evidence labels are the most important design choice on every slide.
